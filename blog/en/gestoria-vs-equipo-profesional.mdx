## Introduction

If you have researched Paraguayan relocation services, you have encountered the word "gestoría." It sounds like a professional service. In practice, a gestoría is something more limited — and understanding the difference is critical to choosing the right support for your move.

## What Is a Gestoría?

A gestoría (from "gestor" — manager or facilitator) is essentially a document-running service. A gestor knows the physical locations of government offices, the opening hours, which window number to approach, and which forms to fill. Their core value is logistical: they save you from standing in line at Migraciones, the police records office, or the tax authority.

Gestores are individuals or small shops that operate on relationships with lower-level administrative staff. They know the clerk who processes residency applications, or the notary who can squeeze you in without an appointment. This is genuinely useful — navigating Paraguay's bureaucracy without local knowledge is frustrating and slow.

However, a gestoría has significant limits. A gestor is not a lawyer. They cannot provide legal advice, draft corporate bylaws, structure a tax plan, or review a real estate purchase contract. They are facilitators, not advisors.

## What a Multidisciplinary Team Provides

A professional team like Nexa Paraguay includes lawyers, accountants, sworn translators, notaries, and client managers working together. This is not a one-person operation with a network of subcontractors. It is an integrated system where each professional handles their domain:

- **Lawyers** handle corporate incorporation, residency applications, contract review, and legal due diligence on property
- **Accountants** manage RUC registration, tax filings, ongoing compliance, and financial planning
- **Sworn translators** handle all document translations with official registration at the Corte Suprema
- **Client managers** coordinate the logistics, timeline, and communication across all domains

The gestor runs documents. The professional team designs and executes strategy.

## Why Relational Capital Matters

The most important difference is relational capital at the decision-making level. A gestor knows the clerk. A professional firm has relationships with the bank compliance officer, the senior examiner at Migraciones, the notary who handles complex international transactions.

When a problem arises — a document is rejected, a bank application is flagged, a registry deadline is missed — the gestor escalates to the same clerk. The professional firm makes a phone call to someone who can resolve the issue with a decision. This is not about "shortcuts" or corruption. It is about working with people who have the authority and expertise to solve problems, not just process paperwork.

## Cost Comparison

A gestoría typically charges $200–$500 per procedure. For a full residency application, a gestor might charge $1,000–$2,000. A professional team charges $3,000–$5,000 for the full package including residency, company incorporation, RUC, and bank account setup.

The gestor is cheaper on paper. But consider what you get for the additional investment: legal review of your documents, tax structure design, post-approval support, and — most importantly — someone who can fix things when they go wrong. The gestor hands you the completed form and moves on. The professional team stays with you through the entire process and beyond.

## When Each Makes Sense

A gestoría is appropriate for simple, routine procedures: renewing a driver's license, obtaining a duplicate ID card, filing a straightforward tax return for a long-term resident who already knows the system.

For complex, high-stakes procedures — first-time residency applications, corporate incorporation with foreign shareholders, property purchases requiring title due diligence, tax planning for international income — a professional team is the appropriate choice. The cost difference is modest compared to the consequences of errors.

## Common Mistakes

The most frequent mistake is hiring a gestor for a residency application, then discovering midway that you need legal advice on document legalization, or that the gestor cannot open a bank account for you because they lack the relationship with compliance. You end up paying twice — once for the gestor, then for a professional team to fix what went wrong.

Another mistake is assuming all "lawyers" are the same. Many solo practitioners in Paraguay operate more as gestores than as attorneys. Ask your provider: who specifically will review my documents? Who will handle the bank introductions? Who will be responsible if my residency application is denied?
