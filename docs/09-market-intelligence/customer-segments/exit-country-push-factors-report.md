## Netherlands

**Personal income tax** tops out at 49.5% for Box 1 income above EUR 78,426 (2026). The Netherlands uses a three-box system: Box 1 (employment & home ownership) at progressive rates up to 49.5%, Box 2 (substantial shareholdings >5%) at 24.5%-31%, and Box 3 (savings & investments) currently taxing a *deemed* return on net assets above roughly EUR 57,000.

**Box 3 reform is the primary push factor.** Starting from 1 January 2028, the Netherlands will tax *actual* returns on assets, including **unrealised capital gains** (the "capital growth" regime). This means Dutch taxpayers will owe tax annually on paper gains in stocks, bonds, and crypto even if they haven't sold anything. Parliament approved this in February 2026, though motions were passed asking the government to eventually switch to a realised-gains-only system. Additionally, the government proposed a **"trailing tax"** (December 2024) that could continue taxing former residents after emigration, targeting those moving to low-tax jurisdictions.

**Corporate tax** is 19% on profits up to EUR 200,000 and 25.8% above. **Inheritance tax** ranges from 10%-40% depending on relationship, with modest exemptions for spouses (EUR 680k) and children (EUR 22k). The Netherlands has no general wealth tax but Box 3 functions similarly. Cost of living is approximately 255% higher than Paraguay. The combination of taxing unrealised gains, the proposed trailing tax on emigrants, and the 49.5% top rate is driving significant interest in jurisdictions like Paraguay that offer territorial taxation (0% on foreign income) and no wealth or inheritance taxes.

## Germany

**Personal income tax** uses geometrically progressive rates from 14% to 42%, with a top rate ("Reichensteuer") of 45% above EUR 277,825 (2025). On top of this, the **solidarity surcharge** (Solidaritatszuschlag) of 5.5% applies to high earners (mostly those with taxable income above ~EUR 105,500 for singles), pushing the effective top marginal rate to approximately 47.5%. Employee social contributions add roughly another 20% (shared equally with employers), making total labour costs among the highest in Europe.

**The tax wedge is extreme.** Germany had the 2nd highest tax wedge in the OECD in 2024 at 47.9%, according to the OECD Taxing Wages report. Social contributions continue rising: pension insurance at 18.6%, health insurance at ~14.6% + 1.8% average additional contribution, long-term care at 3.6% (4.2% for childless), and unemployment at 2.6%. The contribution assessment ceilings increased substantially in 2025-2026, capturing more income in social charges.

**Corporate tax** is a combined ~30-33% effective rate (15% CIT + 5.5% Soli + municipal trade tax of 7-17%). However, the CIT rate is legislated to decrease to 10% by 2032. **Inheritance tax** ranges from 7-50% with allowances of EUR 500k for spouses and EUR 400k per child. Germany has no net wealth tax. Cost of living is about 161% higher than Paraguay. Push factors include the high overall tax burden, social contribution rates that fund generous but expensive welfare states, and an increasingly complex tax code. The solidarity surcharge's persistence despite being originally for reunification (1990) remains a psychological irritant.

## Spain

**Personal income tax (IRPF)** combines national and regional rates for a total that can reach **54-56%** in high-tax regions like Valencia and Catalonia. The national top rate is 47% above EUR 300,000, and savings income is taxed at up to 28%. The "Beckham Law" special regime offers a flat 24% for qualifying new residents on Spanish income up to EUR 600,000, but this is time-limited.

**Spain's wealth tax system is uniquely aggressive among EU countries.** Tax residents pay **0.2%-3.5% on worldwide net assets** above a EUR 700,000 exemption (plus EUR 300,000 for the primary residence). The **Solidarity Tax on Large Fortunes** (Impuesto Temporal de Solidaridad de las Grandes Fortunas), introduced in 2022 and extended, adds 1.7%-3.5% on net assets above EUR 3 million. This effectively creates a national minimum wealth tax floor even in regions like Madrid that offer 100% regional wealth tax relief. The combined wealth tax + Solidarity Tax can result in significant annual costs for high-net-worth individuals.

**Corporate tax** is 25% standard (15% for new companies first two profitable years). **Inheritance tax** varies enormously by region: Madrid and Andalusia offer near-0% effective rates, while Catalonia and Valencia tax heavily (up to 36% in some cases). The national scale is 7.65%-34%. Cost of living is about 141% higher than Paraguay. Push factors include the worldwide wealth tax, the solidarity surcharge making regional tax planning less effective, high income tax rates (up to 56%), and the recent end of the Golden Visa program.

## France

**Personal income tax** reaches a top marginal rate of 45% above EUR 180,294 (2026). On top of this, the **Contribution Exceptionnelle sur les Hauts Revenus (CEHR)** adds 3% on income between EUR 250k and EUR 500k and 4% above EUR 500k for single filers, pushing the effective top rate to approximately **49%**. Social contributions (CSG at 9.2%, CRDS at 0.5%) add further burden on most income types.

**The exit tax is the central push factor for departing residents.** France's exit tax (Article 167 bis CGI) applies when a French tax resident leaves the country after at least 6 of the last 10 years of residence, holding securities with unrealised gains exceeding **EUR 800,000** or a 50%+ shareholding. The tax is levied at 30% flat rate (or progressive rates if more favourable) on the unrealised gain at departure. As of January 2025, the relief period was shortened: the tax is discharged after **2 years** (if securities value below EUR 2.57M) or **5 years** (above that threshold), rather than the previous more generous timeline. Deferral is automatic for EU/EEA moves but requires guarantees for non-EU destinations.

**Wealth tax (IFI)** applies only to real estate assets above EUR 1.3 million at rates of 0.5%-1.5%. This replaced the broader ISF in 2018. **Corporate tax** is 25% standard (15% on first EUR 42,500 for SMEs), but large companies (turnover >EUR 1B) face a temporary surcharge taking the effective rate to 30.975%-36.125%. **Inheritance tax** ranges from 5-45% with a EUR 100,000 allowance per child. Cost of living is roughly 158% higher than Paraguay. The exit tax, combined with high ongoing taxation (49% top rate, IFI), drives interest in territorial tax jurisdictions.

## United Kingdom

**Personal income tax** rates are 20% basic, 40% higher, and **45% additional rate** above GBP 125,140 (2025/26). The personal allowance of GBP 12,570 is tapered away for incomes above GBP 100,000. Scotland sets its own rates (top rate 48% above GBP 75,000).

**The non-dom abolition (April 2025) is the biggest single trigger** driving high-net-worth emigration. The UK's centuries-old remittance basis for non-domiciled residents was replaced with a residence-based system that offers **only 4 years** of relief on foreign income and gains (FIG) for new arrivals (after 10 years of non-residence). Previously, non-doms could shield foreign income from UK tax for up to 15 years. From year 5 onwards, worldwide income and gains are fully taxed on the arising basis. Furthermore, the 2024 Budget extended **inheritance tax (IHT)** to worldwide assets of long-term UK residents, eliminating trust structures that previously protected foreign assets. IHT stands at **40%** above the GBP 325,000 nil-rate band (+GBP 175,000 residence nil-rate). The non-dom population has dropped by ~37%, and an estimated 10,800 millionaires left the UK in 2024 alone.

**Corporate tax** is 19% (small profits rate for profits up to GBP 50,000) and **25%** main rate (for profits above GBP 250,000). Cost of living is about 257% higher than Paraguay. Push factors include: non-dom regime abolition making the UK unattractive for globally mobile wealthy individuals, 40% IHT now catching worldwide assets, the 45% top income tax rate, and a broader anti-wealth sentiment in fiscal policy (private school fees VAT, tighter carry rules). The UK's loss of "non-dom" status has been a boon for competing jurisdictions like Italy, Greece, Switzerland, UAE, and increasingly Paraguay.

## Belgium

**Personal income tax** uses progressive federal rates of 25% (up to EUR 16,320), 40% (EUR 16,320-EUR 28,800), 45% (EUR 28,800-EUR 49,840), and **50%** (above EUR 49,840). On top of this, **municipal surcharges** (communal tax) of 0-9% are applied to the federal tax, pushing the effective top marginal rate to approximately **54.5-59%**. This is before social contributions.

**Belgium has the highest tax wedge in the OECD at 52.5%** for an average single worker (2025 OECD Taxing Wages). Employer social security contributions are approximately 25% and employee contributions approximately 13.07%, creating an enormous gap between gross labour cost and net take-home pay. The tax wedge means that for every EUR 100 an employer pays for an average worker, the worker takes home less than EUR 48. This is the worst in the developed world and a constant source of emigration motivation, particularly among skilled professionals, entrepreneurs, and self-employed individuals.

**There is no general wealth tax** in Belgium, though a 0.15% tax on securities accounts above EUR 1 million exists. **Corporate tax** is 25% standard (20% reduced rate for SMEs on first EUR 100,000 profit). **Inheritance tax** varies by region: Flanders up to 27% (direct line), Brussels/Wallonia up to 30% (direct line). For non-relatives, rates can reach 70-80% in Brussels/Wallonia. Cost of living is about 163% higher than Paraguay. Push factors centre overwhelmingly on the labour tax wedge (highest in OECD), 50% top rate plus municipal surcharges, high social contributions, and a growing sense among professionals that their work is excessively taxed to fund an elaborate welfare state. French-speaking southern Belgians (Wallonia) also look at France's exit tax as a cautionary tale.
